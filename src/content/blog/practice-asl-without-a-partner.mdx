---
title: How to Practice ASL Without a Signing Partner
---

Most people learning ASL do not have anyone to sign with. No Deaf friends yet, no class, nobody at home who knows a single sign. That is a real limitation and you should work on it, but it is not a reason to wait. A lot of fluency is built alone, and some of it is built better alone. Here is how.

## Narrate your day

The single best solo habit. Sign what you are doing as you do it, quietly, to nobody. <S id="morning" />. <S id="coffee" />. <S id="eat" />. <S id="work" />. <S id="tired" />. <S id="home" />. <S id="sleep" />.

<Signs ids="morning,coffee,work,home,night,sleep" columns={3} />

It builds vocabulary in context, which is where it sticks, and it gets your hands used to producing signs without a conscious lookup step. Ten minutes spread across a day is plenty. Nobody has to see it.

## Do receptive drills

Reading signs is the skill that lags and the one you can practice entirely alone. Watch a clip, name the sign, check. Watch another. The practice widget on every [guide](/learn) does exactly this with the signs from that guide, and the Ziggy app does it with spaced review across everything you have learned.

Aim for a third of your practice time on recognition. Beginners who only produce end up with a vocabulary they cannot read back.

## Mirror work for the face

Eyebrows, head tilts, head shakes, puffed cheeks. These are grammar, and you cannot feel whether you are doing them. A mirror tells you. Spend five minutes a day running through [question words](/learn/asl-question-words) with the eyebrows, [feelings](/learn/asl-feelings) with the face, and negatives with the head shake, exaggerated on purpose.

## Record yourself

Once a week, film yourself signing a short paragraph: who you are, what you did today, what you want tomorrow. Watch it back against the dictionary clips. You will see the things a mirror cannot show you: signing too low, bouncing hands, a face that goes blank the moment you concentrate. This is the closest you can get to feedback without a partner.

## Watch real signers, a lot

Deaf creators on video platforms, Deaf news, interpreted events, ASL storytelling. Watch without trying to understand everything. Your eyes are learning to read fingerspelling and faces and the rhythm of real conversation, and that learning happens even when you catch only a fraction of what was said.

Pick two or three Deaf creators whose signing you like and watch them regularly. Over a month you will understand noticeably more, without studying.

## Sign along with clips

Every [dictionary](/dictionary) clip has a half-speed button. Watch, then sign along at half speed, then at full speed. Shadowing, the way language learners repeat along with audio, works for ASL too. It trains the movement and rhythm, which are the parts that drawings and descriptions miss.

## Build sentences, not lists

After each set of signs, make three sentences. Learned [family](/learn/asl-family-signs)? Sign <Gloss>MY SISTER OLD 12, SHE HAPPY</Gloss>. Learned [food](/learn/asl-food-signs)? Sign <Gloss>TONIGHT I EAT PIZZA, DRINK WATER</Gloss>. Vocabulary in a sentence is retained far better than vocabulary in a list.

## Then go find people

Solo practice gets you ready for conversation. It does not replace it. When you are a month or two in and can introduce yourself and ask basic questions, go sign with someone:

- Deaf coffee chats and ASL meetups, which exist in most cities.
- Community college ASL classes, usually with Deaf instructors.
- Online tutors, especially Deaf tutors.
- Deaf events, which are generally public and welcoming.

Lead with <S id="imlearningasl" />. Everything you practiced alone will be shaky the first time with a real person, and that is normal. Our [Deaf culture etiquette](/blog/deaf-culture-etiquette-for-beginners) post covers what to expect.
