---
title: Deaf Culture Etiquette for Beginners
---

When you walk into a Deaf space as a hearing learner, you are a guest in a culture that has its own rules. Most of them are simple, all of them make sense once you know the reason, and knowing them will make you welcome. Here are twelve.

## 1. Get attention with your hands, not your voice

You cannot call a name. Wave in the person's line of sight, tap their shoulder or upper arm lightly, flick the lights for a room, or stomp on a wooden floor. All of these are normal. Shouting is useless and tapping someone's head or waving in their face is rude.

## 2. Keep eye contact

Looking away from someone who is signing is the equivalent of putting your fingers in your ears. If you need to look away, say so first, with <S id="wait" /> or a quick explanation. If you are lost, keep looking and ask for a repeat.

## 3. Say you are learning

<Signs ids="imlearningasl,isignalittle" columns={2} />

<S id="imlearningasl" /> is the single most useful phrase you can bring. It sets expectations and signals respect. Deaf people are overwhelmingly generous with honest learners, and many will slow down, repeat, and teach you signs on the spot.

## 4. Ask for repeats without apologizing every time

<Signs ids="signagain,signslow,idontunderstand" columns={3} />

<S id="signagain" /> and <S id="signslow" /> are ordinary sentences, used between fluent signers constantly. Use them freely. What is annoying is nodding along when you did not understand, which wastes everyone's time. Apologize once for being a beginner, then stop apologizing and just do your best.

## 5. Do not grab hands

Touching or holding someone's hands while they sign is like covering someone's mouth. Do not do it, even to "help" position a handshape, unless they have asked you to.

## 6. Walk through, do not wait

If two people are signing and you need to pass between them, just walk through, maybe with a quick <S id="excuseme" />. Standing and waiting for a gap, or ducking dramatically, is the awkward option. Signers are used to people passing through their sightline.

## 7. Talk to the Deaf person, not the interpreter

<Sign id="interpreter" />

When an interpreter is present, look at and speak to the Deaf person. Say "you," not "tell her." The interpreter is a conduit. Do not chat with the interpreter about the Deaf person while they are standing right there, which happens more than you would think.

## 8. Do not invent your own name sign

<Signs ids="namesign,whatisyournamesign" columns={2} />

Name signs are given by Deaf people, usually after they know you. A hearing learner who shows up with a self-assigned name sign is like a new employee who gives themselves a nickname on day one. Fingerspell your name until someone gives you one.

## 9. Deaf is not a bad word

Say Deaf. Do not say "hearing impaired," which most Deaf people dislike because it frames them as broken hearing people. "Hard of hearing" is the term for people who identify that way. The signs for all three are in [Deaf culture basics](/learn/deaf-culture-basics).

<Signs ids="deaf,hearing,hardofhearing" columns={3} />

## 10. Do not assume lip reading

Even expert lip readers catch a fraction of English, because most English sounds look the same on the lips or are invisible. Do not over-enunciate, do not shout, and do not expect someone to read your lips. Sign, write, type, or use an interpreter.

## 11. Be direct

Deaf culture tends to be more direct than hearing American culture. People will tell you that you look tired, that you have gained weight, that your sign was wrong. This is not rudeness; it is a culture where information is shared openly because it is harder to come by. Take it as a kindness and be direct back.

## 12. Long goodbyes are normal

The "Deaf goodbye" is a real thing. Leaving a Deaf gathering can take half an hour of saying goodbye to each person, making one more plan, and getting pulled into one more conversation. It comes from a history where gatherings were rare and precious. Budget for it, and enjoy it.

## The short version

Be visible, be honest, be direct, keep your eyes up, and let Deaf people lead. If you do those things, the rest is details, and someone will teach you the details with a smile. The sign for that applause is <S id="silentapplause" />: hands up, twisting in the air.
