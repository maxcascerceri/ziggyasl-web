---
title: Family Signs in ASL
---

Family signs in ASL are built on one rule, and once you know it, you can almost guess the signs you have not learned yet. Female signs are made at the chin. Male signs are made at the forehead. The handshape and movement carry the relationship; the location carries the gender.

The rule comes from old-fashioned headwear: the forehead was where a man's hat brim sat, the chin was where a woman's bonnet string tied. Nobody wears those anymore, but the signs still remember.

## Mother and father

<Signs ids="mother,father,parents" columns={3} />

<S id="mother" /> is an open hand with the thumb tapping the chin. <S id="father" /> is the same open hand with the thumb tapping the forehead. Same shape, same tap, different location. <S id="parents" /> touches both: forehead then chin, or chin then forehead.

## Grandparents

<Signs ids="grandmother,grandfather" columns={2} />

<S id="grandmother" /> starts as MOTHER at the chin and bounces forward in two small arcs, one generation out. <S id="grandfather" /> does the same from the forehead. Think of the bounce as stepping back a generation.

## Sister and brother

<Signs ids="sister,brother" columns={2} />

<S id="sister" /> starts with an L handshape at the chin (the female spot) and brings it down to rest on the other index finger. <S id="brother" /> starts with the L at the forehead and does the same. The second half of both signs is the sign for SAME, so sister literally reads as "female, same" and brother as "male, same."

<Note title="Teacher's note">
If you forget a family sign, start from the right spot. Chin for a woman, forehead for a man. Even if you get the handshape slightly off, the location alone will usually get you understood, and a Deaf friend will fix the rest.
</Note>

## Aunt, uncle, cousin

<Signs ids="aunt,uncle,cousin" columns={3} />

<S id="aunt" /> shakes an A handshape near the chin. <S id="uncle" /> shakes a U near the forehead. <S id="cousin" /> shakes a C at the side of the head; signing it near the chin or forehead can specify a female or male cousin.

## Niece, nephew, twins

<Signs ids="niece,nephew,twins" columns={3} />

<S id="niece" /> twists an N near the chin. <S id="nephew" /> twists an N near the forehead. <S id="twins" /> touches a T to one side of the chin and then the other.

## Baby, child, family

<Signs ids="baby,child,family" columns={3} />

<S id="baby" /> rocks your arms as if holding one. <S id="child" /> pats the air at a child's height; patting twice at two heights means children. <S id="family" /> starts with two F handshapes touching and circles them around to meet again, the same shape as GROUP and CLASS with an F for family.

## Talking about your family

A typical first exchange: <Gloss>YOU HAVE SISTER BROTHER?</Gloss> with raised eyebrows. Answer: <Gloss>I HAVE SISTER TWO, BROTHER ONE.</Gloss> Numbers come after the noun. To say how old someone is, sign the relative, then OLD and the number from the chin: <Gloss>MY SISTER OLD 12</Gloss>.

You will also want <S id="married" />, <S id="husband" />, and <S id="wife" /> from the [Relationships](/dictionary/relationships) and [People](/dictionary/people) categories, which follow the same chin-and-forehead logic.

## Practice

Sign your own family tree out loud, one person at a time: MOTHER, FATHER, SISTER, and so on, pointing to a spot in the air for each. Then add their names by fingerspelling. That is both a vocabulary drill and the pronoun setup you learned in [Pronouns](/learn/asl-pronouns).
