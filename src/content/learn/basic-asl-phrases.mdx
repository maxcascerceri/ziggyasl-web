title: Basic ASL Phrases
---

A phrase is a few signs that always travel together. In ASL, phrases drop the English filler. "My name is Ziggy" becomes <Gloss>MY NAME</Gloss> and a fingerspelled name. "I don't understand" is two signs. You learn the phrase as one rhythm, and that rhythm is what makes you sound fluent instead of like someone reading a dictionary out loud.

These twenty-five phrases cover an entire first conversation: meeting, answering, asking for help, apologizing, and saying goodbye. Each clip is filmed as one smooth unit. Click any phrase to see the individual signs inside it.

## Meeting someone

<Signs ids="hello,mynameis,nicetomeetyou,howareyou" columns={2} />

Start with a wave, then <S id="mynameis" />. Fingerspell your name after it. The reply to <S id="howareyou" /> is usually <S id="imfine" /> or <S id="imgood" />, and the face does the real answering: a bright face with FINE means great, a flat face with FINE means tired, leave me alone.

<Signs ids="imfine,imgood" columns={2} />

## The polite set

<Signs ids="thankyou,yourewelcome,please,sorry,excuseme" columns={3} />

<S id="thankyou" /> starts at the chin and moves toward the person you are thanking. <S id="yourewelcome" /> is a sweep that opens toward them. <S id="please" /> is a flat hand circling on the chest. <S id="sorry" /> is a fist circling on the chest with an apologetic face. <S id="excuseme" /> brushes the fingertips across the palm and is also how you get past someone in a crowd.

## Yes, no, and I don't know

<Signs ids="yes,no,dontknow" columns={3} />

<S id="yes" /> nods a fist. <S id="no" /> snaps the index and middle fingers down onto the thumb, with a head shake. <S id="dontknow" /> touches the forehead and flips the hand away. All three carry their meaning as much in the head as in the hand.

<Note title="Teacher's note">
The single most useful thing you can do as a beginner is tell people you are a beginner. Deaf people are generous with learners who are honest about it, and they will slow down, repeat, and often teach you a sign on the spot.
</Note>

## When you are stuck

<Signs ids="imlearningasl,isignalittle,signslow,signagain" columns={2} />

<S id="imlearningasl" /> is your opener in any Deaf space. <S id="isignalittle" /> sets expectations. <S id="signslow" /> and <S id="signagain" /> are not rude; they are the same as saying "pardon?" in English, and people will use them with you too.

<Signs ids="idontunderstand,whatdoesthatmean,howyousignthat" columns={3} />

<S id="idontunderstand" /> is honest and expected. <S id="whatdoesthatmean" /> and <S id="howyousignthat" /> turn every conversation into a lesson. Ask them often; nobody minds.

## Getting help and getting around

<Signs ids="canyouhelpme,wherebathroom" columns={2} />

<S id="canyouhelpme" /> lifts the HELP sign toward the other person with a questioning face. <S id="wherebathroom" /> is the bathroom sign, a shaken T, followed by WHERE with lowered eyebrows. It is the one phrase everyone needs in every building.

## Saying goodbye

<Signs ids="seeyoulater,havegoodday" columns={2} />

<S id="seeyoulater" /> moves the SEE sign forward. <S id="havegoodday" /> is GOOD followed by DAY, and it is a warm way to end any exchange.

## How to practice phrases

Phrases are rhythm. Watch the clip, then sign along with it at full speed three times, even if you are sloppy. Speed first, accuracy second, is the right order for phrases because the connections between the signs are the part your hands need to learn.

Then try it in context. Sign <S id="hello" />, <S id="mynameis" />, your name, <S id="nicetomeetyou" /> as one continuous turn. That is a real introduction, and you can do it today.
