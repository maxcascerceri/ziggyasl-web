title: Deaf Culture Basics
---

ASL is not a code for English. It is the language of a community with its own history, norms, humor, and ways of being polite. Learning the signs without learning the culture is like learning Spanish vocabulary and then being surprised that people greet each other with a kiss on the cheek. This guide covers what every hearing beginner should know before their first signed conversation, and gives you the signs to talk about it.

## Deaf with a capital D

<Signs ids="deaf,hearing,hardofhearing" columns={3} />

<S id="deaf" /> touches an index finger to the ear and then the mouth. <S id="hearing" /> circles an index finger in front of the mouth, which comes from the idea of someone who talks. <S id="hardofhearing" /> bounces an H handshape to the side.

In writing, "Deaf" with a capital D refers to people who identify with Deaf culture and use ASL; "deaf" with a small d is the audiological fact of not hearing. Many Deaf people do not see deafness as a disability but as a language minority identity. You do not need to agree or disagree. You do need to know the distinction exists.

## ASL and sign language

<Signs ids="asl,signlanguage,deafculture" columns={3} />

<S id="asl" /> is fingerspelled A-S-L. <S id="signlanguage" /> is SIGN followed by LANGUAGE. <S id="deafculture" /> is DEAF followed by CULTURE, a C circling in front of the body.

ASL has its own grammar, which you can read about in [ASL grammar vs English](/blog/asl-grammar-vs-english). It is also not universal: British Sign Language is a different language with a different alphabet. See [ASL vs BSL](/blog/asl-vs-bsl) and [is sign language universal](/blog/is-sign-language-universal).

## Getting attention

You cannot call a Deaf person's name. Instead: wave in their line of sight, tap their shoulder lightly, flick the lights in a room, or stomp on a wooden floor so they feel it. All of these are normal. None of them is rude. Grabbing someone's hands while they sign, however, is like putting a hand over someone's mouth.

## Eye contact and attention

Eye contact is not optional. Looking away while someone is signing is the same as walking out of the room mid-sentence. If you need to look away, say so. If you are learning and get lost, keep looking and sign <S id="signagain" /> or <S id="signslow" />.

<Signs ids="imlearningasl,isignalittle,fluent" columns={3} />

The best thing you can do in a Deaf space is sign <S id="imlearningasl" /> early. People are almost always generous with honest learners. <S id="isignalittle" /> sets expectations, and <S id="fluent" /> is what you are working toward.

<Note title="Teacher's note">
Do not apologize for being a beginner more than once. Say it once, then do your best. Over-apologizing puts the Deaf person in the position of comforting you, which is tiring. Confidence and effort are what people respond to.
</Note>

## Name signs

<Signs ids="namesign,whatisyournamesign" columns={2} />

A name sign is a personal sign that stands in for fingerspelling your name. <S id="namesign" /> is NAME followed by SIGN. <S id="whatisyournamesign" /> is how people will ask you.

Name signs are given by Deaf people, usually after they know you. Hearing learners should not invent their own. Until you have one, your fingerspelled name is your name, and that is completely normal.

## Interpreters, captions, and technology

<Signs ids="interpreter,caption,hearingaid,lipread" columns={4} />

<S id="interpreter" /> is INTERPRET plus the PERSON ending. <S id="caption" /> shows text appearing across a screen. <S id="hearingaid" /> hooks a bent finger behind the ear. <S id="lipread" /> circles a bent V in front of the mouth.

When an interpreter is present, speak to the Deaf person, not the interpreter. Look at them, address them, and let the interpreter do their work in the background. Lip reading is not a reliable way to communicate; even expert lip readers catch a fraction of English, so do not expect it.

## Applause

<Sign id="silentapplause" caption="Deaf applause" />

<S id="silentapplause" /> raises both open hands and twists them in the air. It is how a room of signers claps, because it can be seen from anywhere. If you are ever at a Deaf event, this is what you do.

## Practice

Sign the introduction you would give at a Deaf meetup: <S id="hello" />, <S id="imlearningasl" />, <S id="mynameis" /> and your name, <S id="nicetomeetyou" />. Then practice asking <S id="signslow" /> without a sheepish face. You are ready to go.

Then read [Deaf culture etiquette](/blog/deaf-culture-etiquette-for-beginners) on the blog for the longer list, and [how to practice without a partner](/blog/practice-asl-without-a-partner) for what to do between conversations.
