---
title: How to Learn Sign Language
---

You don't need a class, a partner, or a big block of time to start learning sign language. You need the right order, a few minutes a day, and real video of real signers. Here is the path that works for most beginners, from your first letter to your first conversation.

## 1. Choose the right sign language

Sign languages aren't universal. ASL is used in the United States and much of Canada; the UK uses British Sign Language, Australia uses Auslan, and so on. Learn the one used by the Deaf people you want to talk to. [Is sign language universal?](/blog/is-sign-language-universal)

## 2. Learn the alphabet first

Fingerspelling lets you spell any word you don't have a sign for yet, including your name. Most people learn all 26 letters in a few days. Start with our [ASL alphabet guide](/learn/asl-alphabet), then [see your own name fingerspelled](/learn/how-to-sign-your-name).

## 3. Learn your first 50 signs

Start with the signs you'll use in your first conversation: <S id="hello" />, <S id="nicetomeetyou" />, <S id="please" />, <S id="thankyou" />, <S id="sorry" />, <S id="yes" />, <S id="no" />, and <S id="understand" />, plus pronouns and question words. Learn them in groups that go together, not one at a time. [The first 50 ASL signs to learn](/blog/first-50-asl-signs)

## 4. Practice a little every day

Ten minutes a day beats an hour on Sunday. Short daily sessions, with review right before you'd forget a sign, are how vocabulary sticks. An app like Ziggy builds this in: short lessons, a path that knows what's next, and spaced review.

## 5. Learn the grammar early

ASL isn't English on the hands. It has its own word order, and your face is part of the grammar: eyebrows down for who, what, and where questions, eyebrows up for yes/no questions. [ASL grammar is not English](/blog/asl-grammar-vs-english) · [Facial expressions in ASL](/blog/facial-expressions-in-asl)

## 6. Watch real signing

Always learn signs from video, never from still drawings: movement is half the sign. Look up any word in our [free ASL dictionary](/dictionary), slow it down, and copy the signer.

## 7. Learn about Deaf culture

ASL belongs to the Deaf community. Knowing the basics, like how to get someone's attention and why you shouldn't invent your own name sign, makes every conversation go better. [Deaf culture basics](/learn/deaf-culture-basics) · [Deaf etiquette for beginners](/blog/deaf-culture-etiquette-for-beginners)

## 8. Sign with real people

This is where learning turns into language. Take a class (ideally with a Deaf teacher), book a tutor, or go to a local ASL meetup or Deaf community event as a respectful guest. No partner yet? [How to practice ASL without a signing partner](/blog/practice-asl-without-a-partner)

## A 30-day starter plan

| Week | Focus | Daily time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Alphabet, your name, hello and thank you | 10 minutes |
| 2 | Numbers 1–20, pronouns, yes/no, please and sorry | 10 minutes |
| 3 | Question words, feelings, family | 10–15 minutes |
| 4 | Put it together: introduce yourself, ask simple questions, attend one class or meetup | 15 minutes |

## How long does it take?

You can sign a basic introduction in your first week. A slow, friendly conversation takes a few months of daily practice. Fluency takes years, as with any language. [How long does it take to learn ASL?](/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-asl)

## Mistakes to avoid

Signing in English word order, keeping a blank face, bouncing while you fingerspell, and learning from pictures instead of video. [10 common beginner mistakes](/blog/common-beginner-mistakes-asl)
